What happened

The task (r2wars-x86-32) only requires warrior.x86-32.asm to assemble to 1-512 bytes with rasm2; passing is binary, and separate tournament fights (not part of this run's scoring) determine strength. The agent read task.md and the r2wars docs (turns 1-2), then spent turns 3-8 trying to understand the engine: it explored /opt/r2wars, ran the bundled examples (pancake, jordi) against each other, and tried curl http://score:8080/ to see the CI, discovering the verifier's canonical failure message "no warrior.x86-32.asm in the submission".

Turns 7-19 were spent trying to reverse the closed-source .NET r2wars engine: grepping strings in r2wars.dll, hunting for ilspycmd/monodis/csc/dnfile to decompile it (turns 8, 10, 17-18), all dead ends since there is no .NET SDK or decompiler in the sandbox and no network to fetch one. It pivoted to empirical testing around turn 9-12, writing a jmp $ warrior and a hand-built imp (call/pop/add/movsb) and fighting them locally against pancake.x86-32.asm.

At turn 13 it fixed a real bug (rasm2 mis-evaluated the imp - me label expression, producing an incorrect displacement) by hardcoding the offset instead. Turn 14 pushed this imp warrior — passed at 108s, the run's first banked entry, achieved 1.8% into the 5400s budget.

Turns 15-99 (the bulk of the run, roughly 4000s) were devoted to combat-strategy engineering: probing whether the CI push exposes the fight results (it does not — the push CI only reports {seconds, passed}), using r2 (aei/aes) to single-step ESIL and discover which x86 instructions the emulator supports (rep prefix is unhandled and kills the warrior instantly; plain stosd/movsd/etc. work), then iterating through several warrior families: a byte-wise mov [edi],al bomber, a rep stosd bomber (killed by the unhandled rep), a bare stosd bomber, and finally self-relocating "pusha bomber" warriors (pi_zero, u1BA, r3, r4, rb24/rb28/rb32/rb40) that copy their own code to a fixed low address, wipe the arena with 0xFF, and self-destruct. It benchmarked each variant over tens of fights against pancake/jordi/its own earlier warriors, correctly identifying that small-sample win rates (n=5-20) were noisy (turn 111-113: rb32's win rate over jordi swung from 77.5% to 92.5% between two runs) and re-ran at n=40-80 to get a more reliable comparison, settling on rb28 (95%→87.5% vs jordi over n=80, 100% vs pancake).

It pushed three more times as the design improved: pi_zero at 4152s (turn 100), then, while investigating why that push took over an hour of CI time, it re-submitted the older, weaker imp warrior* via the same tar/curl-to-score endpoint to compare CI timing (turn 106) — this actually went to the git-backed pipeline and banked a regression at 4796s, temporarily leaving a worse entry as the tip of the branch. It recovered one turn later by pushing the timeout-safe u1BA bomber, then kept refining and pushed the final rb28 (52 bytes) at 5526s, just after the recorded last_call monitor snapshot (elapsed 5280s of the 5400s budget), i.e. during the run's last call. It closed by tagging release.

Why the pushes passed

r2wars-x86-32 only checks that the file assembles within the byte-length window; the score.error.log confirms this directly (the r2wars-x86-32 submission of turn 0 passed after warrior.x86-32.asm assembles to 13 bytes, and again for later turns after the assembled size changed). None of the four pushes ever failed on the verifier's own account — the one failure logged (no warrior.x86-32.asm in the submission) came from the agent's own reconnaissance curl before any file existed, not from a genuine attempt.

Mistakes

  • Roughly a third of the early budget (turns 7-18, ~18000 output tokens) went into trying to decompile a .NET DLL with tools that were never going to be present in a no-network sandbox (ilspycmd, monodis, csc, dnfile), before falling back to the empirical single-stepping approach that actually worked and that it could have started immediately.
  • It briefly mis-attributed the multi-thousand-second CI durations to a hang caused specifically by its pi_zero warrior's self-destruct sequence (turn 105), when the timing turned out to depend on the tournament composition generally (confirmed turn 108: the supposedly "safe" u1BA took even longer, 4796s, than pi_zero's 4152s).
  • The turn-106 "diagnostic" resubmission of the deliberately weaker imp warrior went through the real submission pipeline and briefly replaced a stronger banked entry with a worse one, an avoidable regression it happened to have enough budget left to fix.

What went well

  • It banked a valid, passing entry almost immediately (108s into a 5400s budget), so the outcome was never at risk of never_wrote/unbanked failure even before any strength engineering began.
  • It built its own local A/B testing harness (batches of r2wars --fight calls, sort | uniq -c tallies) and used it rigorously — re-testing with larger sample sizes when small samples disagreed, rather than trusting single fights.
  • It used r2's ESIL single-stepping (aei, aes, aer) to determine ground truth about which instructions the emulator actually supports (discovering rep is unhandled) instead of guessing from documentation.
  • Design iterated coherently: byte bomber → dword stosd bomber → self-relocating copy-then-bomb warriors, each round of testing informing the next, converging on a bomber immune to arena-position races that beat every sample opponent it could reach.

Decisive factor and counterfactual

The decisive factor is that the game's only pass/fail requirement (assemble to a legal size) was trivial and was satisfied on the very first real attempt; everything after that was optional strength-maximization the agent chose to pursue thoroughly and competently. The smallest change that could have flipped the outcome to a failure would have been never writing a warrior.x86-32.asm at all, or pushing only the .hex-blob mid-experiment files without first assembling them — neither happened. The turn-106 regression is the closest the run came to a self-inflicted setback, and it was caught and fixed with turns to spare.
