Task

chess-vm asks for bot.cvm, a single-decision move-scoring policy in a restricted assembly language (16 registers, no memory, no stack, no .search, capped at 256 instructions / 1024 data words / 20M cycles). The engine scores every legal move once and plays the highest scorer; the verifier plays 20 games per opponent against 27 fixed-strength CVM opponents and reports an Elo-style rating.

What the agent did

  1. Orientation. Read task.md and SPEC.md, checked what tools were installed, and got CI baselines for trivial bots (MATERIAL 544, GAIN 541, SEE 684, random 316) to calibrate what a competitive rating looks like.
  2. Reverse-engineering the CVM. Rather than trusting the spec blindly, it built its own C++ CVM emulator and validated its movegen against all six perft reference counts, then wrote a probe tool comparing its emulator's per-instruction values against the real CI engine's fault-channel output on concrete positions (e.g. the opening move b1→c3). This caught semantic mismatches before they could silently corrupt a trained bot.
  3. First bank. Copied a bare see r0 / score r0 / halt (3 instructions) to bot.cvm and pushed it at 1397s — rating 684, matching the SEE-baseline measured in step 1. This was explicitly described as banking a safe result before investing further: "Pushing the hand-made v1 (CI 707.6) first, so the recorded best improves while I build the distilled bot."
  4. Second push. A 31-instruction hand-written policy (mate-in-1 detection, stalemate avoidance, SEE with mobility-restriction and king-approach terms) pushed at 2908s, rating 708.
  5. Lab-trained distillation. Built a match runner with paired-opening, multithreaded self-play and an Elo readout, discovered the real engine's games are capped well under its lab's default 300-ply limit ("No decision at ply 150 or later exists... much shorter than my lab's 300"), fixed the mismatch, then trained a linear evaluator (material, scalar features, moved-piece PST delta, and a (kind, attackers, defenders) scan) via gradient descent and wrote a codegen script (codegen.py) translating the fixed-point weights into CVM assembly.
  6. Debugging. Hit and fixed a build failure in student.hpp where makeStudent parsed evaluator fields that didn't exist as locals — a normal iteration bug, caught by its own build/test loop rather than surfacing in a push.
  7. Third push. The distilled evaluator (176/256 instructions, 748/1024 data words, ~5k of 20M cycles) pushed at 3330s, rating 984 against a field capped at 1201 — the largest single jump of the run.
  8. Remaining time. Continued training and ablating an opponent-capture-threat feature (estimated to save ~50 instructions per the agent's own ablations) but ran out of time before distilling and pushing it. At the last call it confirmed the working tree matched origin/task and re-ran CI unrecorded to confirm the pushed bot still passed at 983.77, then reported honestly that the extra feature never left lab code.

Why the pushes passed

All three recorded pushes (score.log: 1397/684 pass, 2908/708 pass, 3330/984 pass) succeeded because each bot.cvm produced a score for every legal decision within the cycle budget — the verifier only fails a submission that faults or exhausts its cycle budget. The agent's own CI dry-runs (ci.sh bots/v1.cvm, ci.sh bots/s0.cvm) before each commit meant no push was a guess.

Verdict

A clean, well-instrumented success. The agent treated correctness of its own tooling (emulator, movegen, instruction semantics) as something to prove against the real system rather than assume from the spec, banked incremental progress instead of risking a single last-minute push, and used its remaining budget productively even though the final improvement (capture-threat feature) didn't make it to a fourth push. Nothing here reflects an environment or harness problem — the outcome was determined entirely by the quality of the agent's own evaluator-training and code-generation work.
