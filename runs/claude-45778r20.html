Analysis

Task and pass bar

r2wars-x86-32 only requires warrior.<architecture>.asm to assemble via rasm2 to between 1 and 512 bytes; passing is binary and unrelated to combat strength, which only affects the tournament fights run afterward. All four of the agent's pushes (4227s, 4450s, 4546s, 5320s) passed instantly for this reason — the game is easy to pass and hard to win, and the agent treated it as the latter from the start, using the push-pass bar only as a gate and combat performance as the real objective.

Design arc

  1. Exploration phase (pre-4227s). The agent used raw rasm2/ESIL sessions to check assembler syntax and the HLT (0xf4) death hook before writing any warrior, per its own text: "Now let's design a warrior. First, let me verify the assembler syntax for the pieces I need." and "HLT reliably triggers a death hook... That's enough confirmation. Moving on to writing the actual warrior."
  1. rasm2 label bug. It hit and diagnosed a real assembler quirk: "Found a real rasm2 assembler bug — label references resolve to the wrong address (off by the size of the preceding instruction) when the label sits alone on its own line." It iterated through several hypotheses (single vs. multiple references, forward vs. backward, conditional vs. unconditional) before concluding the safe, general rule was to avoid forward references altogether: "use only backward jumps (proven 100% reliable) via a branchless CMOV-based inner check." This is real, unforced debugging discipline, but it consumed a large share of the pre-first-push budget on a problem that a simpler always-backward-jump design (which is what it shipped) would have sidestepped from the start.
  1. First shipped warrior — bomber4 (55 bytes, pushed at 4227s). A call/pop self-locator followed by a pusha-based sweep that poisons both directions of the arena from its own position, using dec ecx / jnz backward loops only. Validated with boundary tests (B=0, B=982) and real combat: "bomber4 shows 100% (6/6) vs jordi and ~50% vs pancake... Given nothing has been submitted yet despite extensive validation, I'll ship bomber4 now as the first submission, then continue iterating." This is the correct call — bank a valid, decent entry rather than continue refining with nothing on the board — and it landed with roughly 22% of the budget still left.
  1. bomber8 (42 bytes, 4450s) and bomber9 (39 bytes, 4546s). Both trimmed instructions (dropping the forward bound-check idea, dropping sub ebx,5) while re-validating combat performance stayed flat (~44-50% vs pancake, 100% vs jordi): "the 1-cycle setup savings is within noise, but it's strictly smaller with no downside." These are safe, well-justified refinements, not blind size-golfing, since combat rate was checked, not just byte count.
  1. rep stosd breakthrough (26 bytes, 5320s, the release). With budget still available, the agent asked a sharper question than "how do I get smaller" — "does rep stosd cost just 1 cycle regardless of repeat count in this ESIL model?" — and confirmed it empirically: "rep stosd with ecx=5 completed its ENTIRE loop... in a single aes step — meaning r2wars would count it as one turn regardless of repeat count."* It then had to fix two self-inflicted overlap bugs (poison-write start offset copy-pasted from an earlier, smaller variant, overlapping its own not-yet-executed tail bytes) before the design was clean, and cross-checked a synthetic ESIL OOB test against real combat after noticing the two disagreed: "only real combat is trustworthy." The final looped-rep stosd bomber (stostest5) measured at 100% vs jordi and 75% vs pancake, a clear step up from bomber9's ~44-50%, and was shipped as the release: "CI score improved from 4546 to 5320 seconds, confirming the rep stosd design is a genuine improvement over bomber9."

Mistakes, all self-corrected

  • Stale leftover comparison value (cmp edx, 87) from an earlier draft carried into a later one.
  • Copy-pasted a poison-write offset from a smaller prior variant into a larger one, causing the warrior to overwrite its own still-unexecuted code (twice, in slightly different forms — bomber1-class overlap, then again in stostest4/stostest5 drafts).
  • Miscounted its own binary size once by treating wc -c hex-string output (2 chars/byte) as raw bytes, doubling a computed offset.
  • Trusted a synthetic single-step ESIL harness for out-of-bounds detection at one point, which silently missed a real overshoot; only caught by cross-checking against actual r2wars --fight combat.

None of these reached a push — the no-record CI check (tar + POST to the score socket) and real combat testing caught every one before a commit went to task.

Pacing

  • Turn/compaction structure: one continuous session with 4 auto-compactions (large context from many ESIL/combat trial transcripts), ending in what monitor.json records as harness turn 2 with last_call: true.
  • First pass banked at 4227/5400s (~78% of budget spent before any recorded pass), but the agent chose deliberately to ship a "good enough" first pass rather than wait, then used the remaining ~22% of the budget for three further, verified improvements.
  • The agent ended the run itself by pushing a release tag at 5329s (elapsed ~5280–5329s per monitor.json/agent.log), rather than being cut off by the clock — a clean, self-paced finish with a validated best entry banked.

Verdict

This is a well-run, agent-attributable pass: no environment or harness failures appear anywhere in proxy.error.log (only benign nginx periodic-task log noise) or score.error.log (four clean "passed" lines matching the four attempts). The agent's own debugging overhead (rasm2 bug, self-overlap bugs, unit confusion) shaped how the budget was spent but never blocked a push or put the outcome at risk, since every draft was checked against the real verifier before being committed.
