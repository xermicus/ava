Task

The chess-vm game asks for bot.cvm, a move-scoring policy in the CVM assembly language: 16 registers, no writable memory, no stack and no search - the engine runs the submitted program once per legal move and plays the highest-scoring one. Submissions are capped at 256 instructions, 1024 data words and 20M cycles per decision, and are rated by playing 20 games against each of 27 built-in opponents (random through 4-ply search).

What the agent did

Setup and tooling (first ~40 minutes). Before writing any evaluation logic, the agent read the SPEC, probed the score endpoint (Only POST is supported), and then invested in infrastructure: a Go chess engine with move generation validated against five standard perft values, a CVM assembler/interpreter implementing the chess-reading instructions from SPEC.md, and a pool of local opponents (random, greedy, SEE-greedy, alpha-beta at several depths) so it could iterate without the 2-4 second round trip of the real server. It explicitly checked that local rankings of five baseline bots matched the server's ordering (316 < 544 < 684 < 823 < 860) before trusting the simulator.

First submission (v1, 1121 at 718s). A threat-scan/PST/king-danger evaluation was written directly in CVM assembly and pushed as the first recorded entry, already well above the crude baselines (860 locally).

Diagnosis and v3 (1181 at 1930s). The agent used the local simulator to replay lost and drawn games, finding two concrete failure modes: getting mated by queen+bishop batteries near its own king, and drawing by repetition while materially ahead. It fixed a design bug where a defended queen attacked by a knight was misjudged safe (a piece-count heuristic instead of least-valuable-attacker), which alone was worth +56 rating (1125 to 1181), and pushed v3.

The decisive change (v4, 1294 at 3308s). Quantifying the repetition draws showed the bot was ahead by +500 to +1700 material in most of them - a large share of lost points, caused by opponents (especially losing searchers) steering into repetition since a one-ply scorer cannot see it coming. The agent's fix was to add a deterministic hash of (ply, from-square, to-square) as small noise added to the score, but only when material is above a threshold (aheadt). This perturbs move choice just enough to break repetition cycles without changing behavior when the game is undecided. A parallel parameter sweep of noisek/aheadt on the real server found noisek=20 at 1273.75, then noisek=50, aheadt=150 at 1267.36, and the agent pushed a tuned variant that scored 1294 - above the strongest built-in opponent (rating 1201), and enough on its own to guarantee a strong result no matter what happened afterward.

The remaining ~5900 seconds (v5 through v8). From 3308s to the end (~9186s total), the agent kept refining: it added an explain command to the simulator to inspect per-move scores, built dedicated endgame-conversion tests (egtest.sh, generating random basic-mate positions such as K+Q vs K, K+R vs K, etc.), found and fixed conversion failures (e.g. an under-tuned mating-drive term), and iterated through v5-v7 (a 'dedicated mating-drive mode' with a king-box containment term) before settling on v8, which dropped the box/centrality term as redundant (enemy king square is constant across our moves). Throughout this phase it ran 16-seed statistical checks via a custom msweep.sh tool, reporting things like mean 1220.4 max 1277.0 sd 30.9 for a leading candidate - i.e., its own measurements showed the search-heavy candidates clustering around a mean of ~1220 with roughly 30 points of server-side noise, below the 1294 already banked. It shipped v8 anyway (rating 1290 at 9180s, per score.log), which came in slightly below the banked best, then tagged and pushed release to end the run voluntarily.

Why the pushes passed or failed

All four graded pushes passed (score.log: 1121, 1181, 1294, 1290) - the agent never introduced a fault, an instruction/data/cycle overrun, or a rejected .search. games.md/benchmarks.md establish that the entry of record is the highest-rated push, newest on ties, not the latest one - so v8's slightly lower 1290 does not undo v4's 1294; the run's recorded rating is 1294, well clear of the field's top opponent (1201).

Assessment

The strong result rests almost entirely on one insight (noise-when-ahead to defeat repetition-seeking) found and verified about a third of the way into the run. The investment in a validated local simulator and statistical (multi-seed) server verification before pushing is good practice for a benchmark with a genuinely noisy scorer (sd~30 observed by the agent itself), and explains why the agent could iterate through eight bot versions without ever pushing a broken or regressed-on-average submission that it knew was worse.

The main inefficiency is pacing: roughly two-thirds of the run (about 5900 of ~9186 seconds) went into endgame-conversion and king-safety tuning whose own sweep results (mean ~1220) never demonstrated an improvement over the 1294 already banked, and the run's very last push regressed slightly (1290) rather than matching or beating it. Since standings take the best push regardless of order, this did not cost the run anything, but it means the back half of the budget did not clearly pay for itself. The final minutes also included a 10-way parallel local scoring check that hit a self-imposed ~40 second timeout on every one of its requests (status 499 in proxy.access.log, all at 2026-09-23T01:22:18) and was abandoned rather than retried, right before the agent tagged release - a minor sign of the same end-of-run time pressure rather than a deliberate check.
