Why it passed

The final heuristic was competently designed for the CVM instruction set — checkmate-in-1 override, material dominance, clamped SEE for exchange safety, mobility differential, and small positional terms — and it beat the untouched stub's rating (316) by roughly double (645). Nothing about the design is remarkable engineering under time pressure; it reads as a standard one-ply evaluation function assembled from the instruction table in SPEC.md, written directly to the file with no draft or refinement step.

What went wrong

Two independent problems combined to make the pass much closer to a failure than the final rating suggests:

  1. Self-inflicted waste: the first 25 minutes were spent proving four times over that an untouched stub scores 316, rather than writing any part of a real policy. The CI endpoint takes minutes per call (round-robin of 20 games × 27 opponents), so each of those four redundant checks was expensive in wall-clock terms for zero information gained after the first one.
  2. Backend outage: the bulk of the run's actual thinking budget (37m45s, essentially all of turns after the fourth CI check) was lost to llm.substrate.dev DNS failures and aborted/404 requests that left the agent unable to make progress, confirmed by the proxy's own error log and by aborted_requests/failed_requests in run.json.

The combination left the agent with, in effect, one write and one push for the entire hour. It never ran its actual submission through the no-record CI endpoint before pushing it live — the only verification in the whole run was of a file it never changed. Had the hand-written policy contained a bug (a fault, a cycle overrun, or simply a weak heuristic), there would have been no time left to detect or fix it: the push landed after the nominal 3600-second budget had already elapsed, saved only by the last-call grace period.
