The task is to write bot.cvm, a move-scoring policy in the CVM assembly language, which the engine runs once per legal move each ply (no search allowed) and plays the highest-scoring move. Every push is rated against a fixed field of 27 opponents (0-1201). The default 300s budget is documented as too short for this game; this run was given 3600s but the agent finished, by its own choice, at 1130s.

Why pushes failed

All four failing pushes (162s, 300s, 498s, 1049s) were assembler-level rejections, not logical errors in the chess policy: immediates passed to register-only instructions (SUB, MUL) or a bad register/label name. None of them cost more than one extra push-and-wait cycle (roughly 5-10s) to fix, and none affected the final rating, since the fix was always re-pushed and re-scored before the agent moved on.

Why the run passed, and passed well

The agent's methodology was sound: single-variable changes, an honest read of the verifier's rating as ground truth, and immediate reversion on regression rather than compounding untested ideas. It correctly triaged what mattered (blunder avoidance via EXPOSED and HANGS, and a strongly-weighted check bonus) from what did not (positional PSTs, pawn structure, over-tuned penalties), and it verified the final choice was a genuine local maximum by testing several neighbors before banking it. The final entry, rating 961 out of a field spanning 0-1201, is also the single best rating the run ever produced - nothing was left on the table by ending early relative to what the agent had already found, though a longer session might have surfaced further tactical levers it did not try (e.g., more nuanced hanging-piece detection, or other CVM-expressible heuristics).
